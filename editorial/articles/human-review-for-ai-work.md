# Human Review That Makes AI Work Useful

Build a review routine that checks accuracy, relevance, and permissions before a draft becomes a real-world action.

## Define what the draft is allowed to do

Before asking AI for help, separate preparation from action. A draft email is different from a sent email. A proposed schedule is different from an accepted appointment. A summary is different from the original record. Name the output you want and the point where a person must make the decision.
For a simple workflow, write three boundaries: the information the system may use, the result it may prepare, and the actions that require review. These boundaries should be understandable to the person operating the workflow. If the process is unclear to people, adding automation can make the confusion happen faster.

## Choose the source of truth

Identify the record you will check against. For an order, that may be the order details. For a project brief, it may be the approved request. An AI-generated summary is not automatically the authoritative record. Keep the original available so you can trace dates, names, and requirements back to their source.
Ask the draft to distinguish supplied facts from suggestions and missing information. If the input does not include a delivery date, the output should flag that gap rather than invent one. Review the facts most likely to change an action: recipients, amounts, dates, commitments, and quoted wording.

## Use a short, repeatable checklist

Create a review checklist with four questions. Does the draft match the request? Are its factual statements supported by the supplied record? Does it include only information appropriate for the recipient? Does the proposed action stay within the authority granted? These questions create a useful baseline for routine work.
Add task-specific checks where necessary. A public announcement may need approved wording. A customer reply may need the correct order number and a realistic promise. Do not bury essential checks in a list so long that nobody can use it. Keep the important checks visible at the decision point.

## Test difficult cases before routine use

Collect a few cases that can expose failure: missing details, contradictory notes, duplicate names, outdated instructions, and an unclear request. Write what a good response should do for each. Often the correct result is a question or a flagged gap, not a completed answer.
Run the workflow on these cases and compare the results with your expectations. Fix the instructions when the same misunderstanding repeats. Keep a record of the change and rerun the relevant case. A demonstration on one clean example can show potential, but it does not show how the workflow handles messy work.

## Make approval an observable step

Give the reviewer the draft, the relevant source, and the proposed next action together. Ask for an explicit decision rather than assuming silence means approval. If the reviewer edits a promise, ensure the final action uses the edited version. Avoid a process where approval refers to one draft while a different draft gets sent.
Record enough information to explain what happened: the final output, who approved it, and the action taken. Store that record according to the needs of your project. You should be able to answer a practical question later: which information did we use, and why did we choose this result?

## Improve the process using real corrections

After several reviewed tasks, look at the corrections. Were dates missing, tone mismatched, or requirements ignored? Turn repeated corrections into clearer input instructions or targeted checks. Do not add broad rules for every isolated preference unless they help future work.
Measure useful outcomes such as review time, recurring errors, and tasks that need clarification. Faster drafting alone may hide extra cleanup. A workflow earns its place when the complete process helps people produce a dependable result with a clear understanding of who decides what.

## Q&A

Can a polished AI draft skip review?

Polish does not establish accuracy or authority. Check the source, recipient, commitments, and permitted action before using the draft.
