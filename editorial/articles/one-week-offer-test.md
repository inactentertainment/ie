# A One-Week Offer Test Before You Build

Turn an idea into a focused customer experiment, with a clear question, a modest budget, and a decision you can explain.

## Choose the decision before the deliverable

A logo, storefront, and product collection can make a business feel real. They do not tell you whether someone needs the offer. Before building the full experience, write the decision you need to make: should I develop this offer further, revise its audience, or stop this version? Your experiment should produce evidence for that decision. It should not become a disguised launch requiring weeks of preparation.
Start with one audience, one situation, and one proposed result. For example: a printable practice planner for volunteer basketball coaches who prepare sessions after work. That is specific enough to discuss with a real person. ‘Resources for everyone who likes sports’ is too broad to reveal what matters. Describe what the customer receives, how they would use it, and what still needs development.

## Day one: write an honest offer

Create a one-page description with a title, a sample, the intended user, and a clear next action. Say whether the product is available, in development, or simply being tested. A request for feedback should look like a request for feedback. A waitlist should explain what joining means. Avoid a checkout button if you cannot reliably fulfill the order under the stated terms.
Choose a signal that fits the question. An interview can reveal the problem and language people use. A request for a sample can show interest in trying something. A purchase can show willingness to pay under those particular conditions. These signals answer different questions. Do not treat a compliment as evidence of a sale, or an email signup as a promise to buy.

## Days two and three: listen to the situation

Invite a small, relevant group to describe how they currently handle the problem. Ask about the last time it happened, what they tried, and where the process became difficult. Let them answer before showing your idea. A leading question such as ‘Would this amazing planner help?’ invites politeness. ‘How did you prepare your last practice?’ gives you a process to examine.
Keep a simple record: situation, current workaround, consequence, and exact words worth remembering. Separate what the person said from your interpretation. Five conversations can help you form better questions; they cannot establish a universal market conclusion. Record who you spoke with so you understand whose experience your experiment represents.

## Days four and five: show one useful sample

Make the smallest sample that lets someone try the central benefit. For a planner, that might be one completed example and one blank page. For a learning tool, it might be one working activity. Tell the participant what to attempt, then observe where they hesitate. Ask what they expected rather than defending your design.
Change one significant part at a time when you want to compare versions. If the audience, price, image, and wording all change, you will struggle to explain the result. With a small group, focus on the quality of observations rather than claiming statistical certainty. A repeated misunderstanding is still a useful design finding.

## Days six and seven: decide what earned another week

Review the evidence against criteria you wrote before the test. A hypothetical rule might be: continue if three relevant participants can use the sample without help and at least two request a fuller version. That is a project decision rule, not an industry benchmark. Record what actually happened, including silence and negative feedback.
Choose a next action with a limit. You might revise one confusing instruction, interview a different audience, or develop one additional page. If people like the appearance but cannot explain the benefit, improve the offer before adding features. If the problem is minor and everyone has a satisfactory workaround, consider another problem. The value of the week is a clearer decision, even when the decision is to stop.

## Your experiment worksheet

Write six lines: audience; recent problem; smallest sample; signal to observe; time and money limit; continue, revise, or stop criteria. Add an evidence column and an interpretation column. After each conversation, enter one observation in each. This keeps your enthusiasm visible without letting it replace what happened.
Finish with a short decision note: ‘We expected this, observed this, and will do this next.’ Include what remains unknown. A good offer test leaves you with a smaller uncertainty and a more purposeful next investment. It does not need to make the whole business certain.

## Q&A

Should I build the whole store to test demand?

Start with a truthful sample and a defined decision. Build more when the evidence identifies a useful next step.
