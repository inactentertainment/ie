# A Portfolio Case Study That Shows Your Thinking

Explain a project through its problem, decisions, evidence, and next iteration so visitors can understand your contribution.

## Start with the problem someone can recognize

A portfolio page should help a visitor understand what you can contribute. Begin with the situation, audience, and desired result. Instead of opening with a list of tools, explain the problem you were trying to solve. Tools become meaningful when the reader knows the job they helped you perform.
Identify the status of the work: client project, personal project, prototype, or classroom exercise. Be clear about your role and collaborators. A prototype can demonstrate thoughtful design without being presented as a launched product. Honest context makes the evidence easier to evaluate.

## Show a few decisions, not every step

Choose two or three decisions that shaped the result. Explain the constraint, the options you considered, and why you chose one. For example, a multistep contact form might reduce irrelevant questions by branching after the service selection. A mobile layout might simplify navigation because the previous menu covered the content.
Use screenshots or examples that help the reader inspect the decision. A caption should say what to notice. ‘The selected service reveals relevant follow-up questions’ teaches more than ‘New design.’ Include a before-and-after comparison when you have a genuine earlier version and a clear reason to compare it.

## Match evidence to the claim

If you say the page is easier to use, show what you observed. Perhaps three test participants completed the task and two previously missed a button. Describe the test and its limits. A small informal test is useful evidence for a local design decision, but it should not be described as a universal result.
When you have no user results, explain what you verified instead: links, layout at specified widths, form logic, or accessibility checks. Label these as implementation checks. Do not invent conversion improvements, revenue, or customer feedback to make a case study sound more impressive.

## Make the finished work easy to explore

Include a clear link to the working example when sharing it is appropriate. Tell the visitor what to try first. A person should not need to search a large site to find the feature your case study discusses. A brief walkthrough can help when the live experience requires several steps.
Keep the main story readable on a phone. Use short sections, legible images, and captions. A screenshot with tiny text is not strong evidence if the reader cannot inspect it. Provide a text explanation of the important interaction so the story survives when an image cannot be viewed.

## Include one thoughtful limitation

Name something you would improve next and why. This should be a real observation rather than a rehearsed weakness. You might need more representative user testing, a clearer empty state, or a better way to recover from a failed submission. Explain what evidence would guide the next iteration.
A limitation does not erase the accomplishment. It shows you understand the difference between a successful prototype, a dependable implementation, and a fully evaluated experience. Readers can better assess your judgment when you describe where the work currently stands.

## Draft your case study in seven lines

Write: situation; intended audience; your role; important constraint; key decision; evidence; next improvement. Expand each line into a short paragraph and add one visual that clarifies the most important decision. Remove tool names that do not help explain the work.
Finish by checking every outcome statement. Can you point to a result, observation, or artifact that supports it? If not, rewrite it as an intention or an open question. A strong case study makes your thinking visible and gives the visitor a reliable reason to start a conversation.

## Q&A

What if my project has no sales or user metrics?

Show the actual work, your decisions, and the checks you completed. Label untested outcomes as goals and explain what you would evaluate next.
