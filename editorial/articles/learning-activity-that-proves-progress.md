# Design a Learning Activity That Proves Progress

Connect a practical outcome, a worked example, an independent task, and feedback that helps learners improve.

## Begin with something a learner can do

A lesson can explain an idea clearly while leaving the learner unable to use it. Start by naming a performance: what should the person produce or decide after this lesson? ‘Understand planning’ gives you little to observe. ‘Create a thirty-minute practice with a warm-up, one skill focus, and an assessment’ gives both learner and teacher a target.
Choose a task that resembles the situation where the skill will matter. If the outcome involves comparing options, let learners compare options. If it involves writing instructions, ask for instructions. A recall quiz can check terminology, but it should not be your only evidence for an outcome that requires making something.

## Show the thinking inside an example

A worked example should reveal choices, not just display a polished answer. Present the starting situation, the constraints, the result, and the reason for two or three decisions. A learner needs to see why a step fits this particular problem. Otherwise the example becomes a pattern to imitate without judgment.
For a practice plan, explain why the opening activity prepares the skill you will teach, why the time limit is realistic, and what observation will show progress. Include one plausible alternative and explain when it might fit better. This gives learners a way to adapt the example instead of copying it blindly.

## Remove one support at a time

After the example, provide a partially completed task. Ask the learner to finish one decision and explain it. Then offer an independent version with a different situation. The progression should increase responsibility without unexpectedly changing every condition at once. Make the directions and success criteria available throughout.
Support can take several forms: a checklist, an annotated example, plain-language definitions, or a starting template. Let learners choose a helpful format when the format itself is not the skill being assessed. If the outcome is reasoning about a plan, a typed response, recorded explanation, or structured diagram may each reveal useful evidence.

## Write a rubric that can guide revision

Use a small set of criteria tied to the outcome. For a plan, you might examine alignment with the skill goal, feasibility within the time available, and a clear way to observe progress. Describe what each criterion looks like in the work. ‘Excellent effort’ is too vague to tell someone what to change.
Separate essential requirements from optional polish. A beautiful page can still contain an impractical plan. A plain page can show sound reasoning. Show the rubric before the task, then ask learners to use it on their own draft. Self-review is stronger when it points to visible evidence instead of an overall feeling.

## Make feedback point to the next action

Useful feedback identifies an observation, explains its consequence, and proposes a revision. ‘Your final activity has no observation step, so you cannot tell whether the skill improved. Add one specific behavior to watch’ gives the learner something to do. Praise can recognize a strength, but it should also name the decision that made the work effective.
Give learners an opportunity to revise. A score without another attempt can turn feedback into a final verdict. Ask for a brief change note explaining what was revised and why. That note can reveal whether the learner understood the feedback or simply followed an instruction.

## Try the design on a small lesson

Select one outcome and draft a twenty-minute experience: a short explanation, an annotated example, a guided attempt, and an independent task. Write three success criteria and one likely misunderstanding. Have someone follow the directions without your help. Record where the directions require an assumption you forgot to explain.
Your final check is simple: does the task produce evidence of the outcome? If the learner can succeed by copying a phrase while avoiding the intended skill, revise the task. Learning progress becomes clearer when the example, activity, and feedback all point to the same practical result.

## Q&A

Is a quiz enough to show someone can apply a skill?

Use a quiz for concepts and a realistic task for performance. Assess the task with criteria tied to the intended outcome.
