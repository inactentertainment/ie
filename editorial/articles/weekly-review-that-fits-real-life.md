# A Weekly Review That Fits Real Life

Build a brief review that reconnects tasks, commitments, and the next week without becoming another oversized project.

## Give the review one job

A weekly review is a chance to see your commitments and choose what happens next. It is not a demand to reorganize your entire life every Sunday. Keep its job narrow: collect loose items, check the coming week, and decide which actions deserve attention. A review that needs perfect conditions will be hard to repeat.
Choose a realistic time limit and a consistent place for your notes. Twenty minutes is an illustrative starting point, not a required standard. If you have five minutes, use the shortened version. The system should help you recover after a busy week rather than treat a missed session as failure.

## Collect before you prioritize

Begin with the places where work accumulates: notes, email flags, messages, paper, and unfinished tasks. Move actionable items into one trusted list. You do not need to copy every piece of information. Save reference material where you can find it and capture only the action that depends on it.
Write actions with a verb and a result. ‘Website’ is a subject. ‘Review the contact form on a phone and record three issues’ is an action. If you cannot describe the next step, put the item in a clarify group. A vague item can consume attention without becoming any easier to begin.

## Check commitments against the calendar

Look backward for promises you made and forward for appointments or deadlines. Include family responsibilities and preparation time. A deadline on Friday may require work on Wednesday; an appointment may need travel or documents. Add these supporting actions before filling the remaining time with new ideas.
Distinguish a fixed commitment from an intention. A calendar event should mean something you have chosen to do at that time. A long list of aspirational calendar blocks can hide the obligations you must protect. Keep your task list available for work that can move within the week.

## Choose a small set of outcomes

Select a few meaningful results you can realistically finish. For each, name the next action and what ‘done’ means. Consider your actual available time, energy, and dependencies. If someone else must provide information, contacting them may be the first action. Do not schedule a final result while ignoring the prerequisite.
Keep a visible ‘not this week’ list. Deferring an idea deliberately is different from losing it. This list lets you protect current work without pretending the other ideas no longer matter. Review it when circumstances change rather than reopening every idea throughout the day.

## Use AI for preparation, then check it

If you use AI, provide a limited, nonsensitive list and ask it to group tasks or identify unclear actions. Treat the response as a draft. It may misread priorities, invent a dependency, or overlook a commitment you never supplied. Confirm dates against your actual calendar and keep the final choices yours.
A useful prompt is: ‘Group these items by project, flag vague actions, and ask questions about missing information. Do not invent deadlines.’ Read the output beside the original list. A tidy summary is only helpful when important commitments survive the cleanup.

## Close with a restart point

End the review by writing the first action for your next work session. Make it small enough to begin without another planning meeting with yourself. Prepare the relevant file, checklist, or link. This creates a bridge between planning and doing.
Try the process for two weeks and notice where it becomes cumbersome. Shorten steps that do not change your decisions. Keep the parts that reveal forgotten commitments or clarify what to do next. The goal is a review you can return to, including after the weeks when life interrupts the plan.

## Q&A

What should I do after missing a weekly review?

Restart with upcoming commitments and one next action. Resume the useful routine without reconstructing every missed session.
