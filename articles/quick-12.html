    Make motion expressive and controllable

    Use bounce and shimmer to guide attention without demanding it.

    InAct Entertainment Editorial · Updated October 9, 2026
    255 words · 2 min read · Practical quick read

    Back to articles
    Your learning route
    Start with the idea

    Start with the idea

    A silver button can feel alive with a small lift, a brief shimmy, and a light sweep. A logo can respond to hover with a soft spotlight. Those effects create character when they reinforce a visitor’s action rather than compete with the content the visitor is trying to read.

    Choose a motion language with a few consistent rules. Buttons respond to interaction. Selected items show a stable state. Background movement stays subtle. Avoid assigning a separate dramatic animation to every element, because the page can quickly lose a clear center of attention.

    MDN documents the prefers-reduced-motion media feature, which lets a website respond to a user’s operating-system preference. Respect that preference by disabling unnecessary animation and retaining clear visual feedback. The destination and meaning of a button should remain understandable even when every moving effect is turned off.

    Check the experience with a keyboard and on a touch screen. Hover is not available everywhere, so important information must not depend on it. Provide visible focus states, comfortable tap targets, and obvious controls for opening and closing a panel. A music icon can animate while playing, but its label should explain what the control opens.

    Review motion as part of the reading experience. Does the shimmer pull your eye away from an article? Does a bouncing item cover a nearby target? Does the page still feel polished when motion is reduced? Good interaction design answers those questions with restraint. The animation is successful when it makes the action feel responsive and the content easier to explore.
